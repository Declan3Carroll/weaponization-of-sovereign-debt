# The Weaponization of Sovereign Debt: China's Belt and Road Restructuring and the Economics of "Non-Contact" Imperialism

**Declan Carroll**


![Hambantota International Port, Sri Lanka (photo: Dilshanhettihewa, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c8/Hambantota_International_Port_View.jpg)

On December 9, 2017, operational control of the deep-water port at Hambantota, on Sri Lanka's southern coast, passed to a joint venture in which China Merchants Port Holdings, a state-controlled Chinese firm, held an 85 percent stake, under a lease with ninety-nine years left to run [1]. In the prevailing telling, this was the moment the Belt and Road Initiative revealed its true face. Vice President Mike Pence, speaking at the Hudson Institute ten months later, compressed the story into its canonical form: Sri Lanka "took on massive debt to let Chinese state companies build a port with questionable commercial value," and when the island could no longer afford its payments, "Beijing pressured Sri Lanka to deliver the new port directly into Chinese hands," where it "may soon become a forward military base for China's growing blue-water navy" [2]. The episode had already given the debate its governing phrase. In January 2017, eleven months before the lease was signed, the Indian strategist Brahma Chellaney had described Beijing's lending as "debt-trap diplomacy": a deliberate strategy of extending credit beyond a borrower's capacity to repay, so that default would deliver strategic assets into Chinese hands [3]. A port had been built, a debt had been incurred, a lease had changed hands, and a superpower had acquired a foothold astride the shipping lanes of the Indian Ocean. The syllogism wrote itself, and within two years it had hardened into conventional wisdom in Washington, New Delhi, Tokyo, and beyond.

There is one problem with this story: nearly every load-bearing element of it is false. The port was not seized. No debt was cancelled in exchange for it. Sri Lanka's debt crisis was not caused by Chinese lending, and the decision to lease Hambantota was initiated in Colombo, not Beijing. The corrective scholarship is now extensive, rigorous, and drawn substantially from Sri Lanka's own government records [4][1]. Yet the correct response to the collapse of the canonical narrative is not complacency. Strip the myth away and what remains is not an innocent development bank but something more subtle and, in the long run, more consequential: a creditor state that has learned to exercise strategic power entirely through the architecture of its loan contracts, the information asymmetries those contracts create, and control over the restructuring processes that follow distress, all without seizing anything at all. The old empires landed gunboats and administrators; the new one arrives with a term sheet. The mechanism is quieter than conquest, deniable where conquest was brazen, and largely invisible to the international legal order that was built to police the older kind. It deserves a name, and this essay proposes one: non-contact imperialism, the acquisition of durable strategic leverage over sovereign states through the design and servicing of debt, exercised at the negotiating table rather than the customs house, and perfected not in the lending itself but in the restructuring.

The scale of the system makes the question worth asking properly. Between 2000 and 2021, Chinese official-sector institutions extended some $1.34 trillion in loans and grants across 20,985 projects in 165 low- and middle-income countries, the largest single bilateral overseas lending program in history [5]. A substantial share of this lending never appeared in the standard statistical record: researchers at AidData, the Kiel Institute, and the Center for Global Development estimate that roughly half of China's overseas lending to developing countries goes unreported to the World Bank's debtor reporting system, leaving the true scale of public debt owed to China systematically understated in the figures on which multilateral surveillance depends [6]. And the contracts themselves differ from those of other sovereign creditors in ways that matter. The most comprehensive study of Chinese loan contracts yet published, an AidData-led analysis of one hundred agreements between Chinese state lenders and twenty-four borrowing governments, found unusually broad confidentiality obligations binding the borrower, collateralization not through legal title but through escrowed revenue accounts in banks acceptable to the lender, clauses committing the borrower to exclude the debt from any Paris Club restructuring, and cross-default provisions linking the loan to the wider bilateral relationship [7]. None of these provisions is a smoking gun. Together they describe a creditor that has engineered, deliberately and at scale, the legal infrastructure of seniority and leverage.

Nowhere is the gap between the myth and the record wider than at Hambantota itself. Sri Lanka's descent into default was made not in Beijing but in the international bond markets. After the island was upgraded to lower-middle-income status in 1997 and gradually lost access to concessional financing, its governments funded an infrastructure-and-consumption growth strategy with commercial borrowing: commercial loans and export credit rose from 3 percent of government external debt in 2000 to 60 percent by 2021, and commercial borrowing alone grew from 4.4 percent of central government external borrowing in 2006 to 44.7 percent in 2021 [1]. By the mid-2010s, according to the IMF, roughly three-quarters of Sri Lanka's external government debt was owed to private creditors [4]. Chinese lending was growing fast over the same period, from under 1 percent of public external debt in 2000 to 19.6 percent by the end of 2021, making China the largest bilateral creditor, but at $7.4 billion of a $37.6 billion total it was never the largest claim, and Chinese loans accounted for a small fraction of the debt-service obligations that crushed the treasury [1]. What crushed it was the wall of maturing international sovereign bonds: foreign reserves fell from $7.6 billion at the end of 2019 to $3.1 billion at the end of 2021 and effectively to zero by March 2022, and on April 12, 2022, Sri Lanka suspended external debt payments and defaulted [1].

The lease that fed the myth inverts every element of the debt-trap account. The proposal came from Colombo: in 2016, facing an acute balance-of-payments squeeze, the Sri Lankan government first approached Japanese and Indian investors about a port partnership, and only after those approaches failed did it lobby in Beijing, where the prime minister asked President Xi Jinping directly for assistance and was refused budget support but offered help finding a commercial investor [4]. The transaction that followed was not a debt-for-equity swap. The Sri Lanka Ports Authority created two companies, sold 85 percent of the port operating company to China Merchants Port for $973.7 million, and retained legal ownership of the port itself; no Sri Lankan debt to China was cancelled, reduced, or even reprofiled as part of the deal, and the proceeds, arriving in tranches of $292 million in December 2017, $97 million in January 2018, and $584 million in June 2018, were used to bolster reserves and service other, largely Western, creditors [4][1]. The 99-year lease was a Sri Lankan policy choice about concession duration, and the security fear that animated Pence's speech was contractually foreclosed: the port cannot host the Chinese navy, and Sri Lanka's southern naval command was relocated alongside it precisely to keep watch [4]. In CARI's measured judgment, the equity injection was a non-debt-creating foreign-currency inflow that eased the very fiscal and balance-of-payments pressures the myth claims it created [1].

And yet it would be a mistake to leave the story where the debunkers leave it. Something did change hands at Hambantota: control of a strategically located port, for ninety-nine years, at a moment of maximal borrower desperation, on terms the borrower sought because no one else would offer them. The number itself carries history. When Britain took the New Territories from Qing China in 1898, the instrument was likewise a 99-year lease, and the Chinese negotiators of that era understood such instruments as humiliations precisely because a lease of a century is ownership in every sense that matters to the people who sign it [8]. That a Chinese state firm now holds a 99-year instrument on foreign soil is not proof of imperial design; leases of this length are a commercial convention in port concessions. But the resonance points at the right question. If the port was not the prize of a debt trap, what exactly did China buy with its $1.34 trillion, and where, if not at Hambantota, does the leverage live? The answer is that the leverage was never in the asset. It is in the contract, in the opacity, and above all in what happens when the bill comes due and every other creditor is forced to negotiate with, around, or against Beijing.

To see the mechanism clearly, begin with the contracts. A conventional sovereign loan from a Western government or multilateral institution is a policy instrument: it prices risk against the borrower's reform program, disburses against conditions, and accepts, as a matter of long-established practice, that in a crisis it will be restructured multilaterally through the Paris Club. A Chinese policy-bank loan is built on a different theory. The Export-Import Bank of China and the China Development Bank lend as collateral creditors. The AidData contract study found that Chinese state lenders routinely require borrowers to hold foreign-currency revenue accounts in banks acceptable to the lender, funded by project receipts and effectively escrowed against repayment; that roughly three-quarters of the contracts examined barred the borrower from seeking comparable treatment alongside Paris Club creditors, thereby walling Chinese claims off from collective restructuring in advance; that confidentiality clauses of unusual breadth prohibit the borrower from disclosing the terms, sometimes even the existence, of the debt; and that many contracts embed the loan in the wider diplomatic relationship through cross-default and cancellation provisions tied to breaches of policy commitments [7]. The effect is a claim that is informationally privileged, contractually senior in practice whatever its legal rank, and enforceable without ever entering a courtroom, because the escrowed revenues sit outside the borrower's control before any dispute begins.

This is the first sense in which the imperialism is non-contact. The classic instrument of creditor control over a defaulting sovereign was the receivership: after the Ottoman Empire's default of 1875, its European creditors secured in 1881 the Ottoman Public Debt Administration, a foreign-staffed bureaucracy inside the Ottoman state that collected the salt, tobacco, silk, fishery, and stamp revenues directly and remitted them to bondholders, employing more officials than the Ottoman finance ministry itself and answerable to creditors rather than to the sultan [9]. The arrangement was an affront to sovereignty so total that its abolition became a founding cause of the Turkish Republic. The escrowed revenue account of a modern Chinese loan contract is the receivership reimagined for an era in which territorial administration is illegitimate: the creditor never staffs an office in the debtor's capital, never collects a tax, never appears to govern, and yet a defined stream of the borrower's foreign earnings is routed, by contract, past the borrower's treasury before the borrower can touch it. Control has been achieved at zero administrative cost and with full formal consent, which is precisely what makes it invisible to the legal categories, occupation, annexation, colonial administration, through which international law recognizes the loss of sovereignty.

The second sense is informational. A creditor whose claims are secret holds an asset no other creditor can price. When half of a borrower's debt to its largest bilateral lender is invisible to the World Bank's statistics [6], every IMF debt-sustainability analysis, every private creditor's restructuring model, and every parliamentary debate in the borrowing country proceeds on a false balance sheet, and the one actor with the true ledger is the lender itself. Information asymmetry of this kind is not a byproduct of Chinese lending practice; it is written into the contracts as an affirmative obligation of the borrower [7]. Its strategic yield became visible in the first great test of the system, the wave of sovereign restructurings that followed the pandemic, in which the central question, in Lusaka, in Accra, in Colombo, and in Addis Ababa, was never whether Beijing would be repaid, but who would move first, on what information, and at whose expense.

That test arrived with the G20's Common Framework, the 2020 initiative meant to extend Paris Club-style collective restructuring to a creditor universe that now includes China. Its first full case was Zambia, which defaulted in November 2020 and spent the next three and a half years in a negotiation whose geometry revealed everything about the new order. China was simultaneously Zambia's largest official creditor and, for the first time in history, the co-chair of its official creditor committee alongside France. The deal announced in June 2023 rescheduled $6.3 billion over more than twenty years with a three-year grace period, at interest rates of 1 percent until 2037 rising to a maximum of 2.5 percent, terms that extended maturities and slashed coupons while leaving principal untouched [10][11]. Five months later the same committee, with China at its head, rejected Zambia's agreement with its private bondholders on comparability-of-treatment grounds, judging the bondholder terms too generous relative to what official creditors had accepted, and forced the private side back to the table [12]. The sequence is worth dwelling on. A creditor that had spent two decades refusing Paris Club membership and writing contractual refusals of collective restructuring into its loan agreements now sat in the chairman's seat of a restructuring club, wielding its veto not to extract better terms for itself but to discipline another creditor class, while its own claims emerged with every dollar of principal intact.

The pattern repeated across the restructuring wave. In Ethiopia, whose official creditor committee was likewise co-chaired by France and China, the committee granted a debt-service standstill through 2023 and 2024, relief in the form of time rather than reduction, while the country's bondholders faced the prospect of principal losses [13]. In Ghana, whose $13 billion Eurobond exchange imposed a 37 percent nominal haircut on private investors, the government bound itself by a most-favored-creditor clause promising that any more favorable terms granted to other creditors would extend to the bondholders, a clause that exists precisely because private creditors no longer trust official bilateral claims, above all China's, to absorb comparable losses [14]. The outcomes admit a simple quantitative summary, and it is the analytic core of this essay: in the great restructurings of 2023 and 2024, private creditors absorbed nominal principal haircuts of 37 percent in Ghana and 28 percent in Sri Lanka, while official bilateral creditors, led in every case by China, absorbed nominal haircuts of zero, accepting longer maturities and lower coupons but preserving the face value of every claim [14][15][10][11] (see Figure 2). The face value matters more than it appears. A claim preserved at par is a lever preserved at full strength: it remains on the books, accruing diplomatic weight, available for the next rollover negotiation, the next refinancing, the next favor.

Sri Lanka supplied the structural counterpoint that proves the rule. There, China declined to join the official creditor committee at all. The committee that formed in May 2023, seventeen countries co-chaired by India, Japan, and France, could list China only among its observers, and Beijing instead cut its own separate deal with Colombo covering $4.2 billion in Export-Import Bank claims, reached in October 2023, a month before the committee's own agreement in principle [16][17]. The committee's statement noted, with diplomatic restraint, that it expected "other bilateral creditors" to share the information necessary to evaluate comparability of treatment [16]. The meaning was plain: the multilateral system could no longer command the participation, or even the transparency, of the largest bilateral creditor to a defaulting sovereign, and could only ask. A creditor powerful enough to choose its forum, inside the committee in Lusaka and Addis Ababa, outside it in Colombo, holds an option that no Paris Club member ever possessed, and it exercises that option case by case, wherever its leverage is greatest.

The restructuring table, however, shows only half of the system. The other half is visible in the countries that never restructure at all, because their relationship with their creditor has passed beyond the event of default into a condition of permanent deference. Laos is the limiting case. By the end of 2024, Laos owed Chinese creditors an estimated $8.6 billion in outstanding public and publicly guaranteed debt, equivalent to 85 percent of its total external public debt and 52 percent of GDP, the highest debt-to-China ratio of any low- or middle-income country; a further $7 billion of exposure sits in the special purpose vehicle of the Laos-China Railway, unguaranteed on paper but far too large for the government to abandon; and 45 percent of all Chinese loan commitments to the country show signs of distress [18]. Yet Laos has never sought the Common Framework, never joined the DSSI, never defaulted. Instead it renegotiates bilaterally and perpetually, having received more than $2.6 billion in Chinese cash-flow relief through deferrals between 2020 and 2024 [18]. Each deferral is a favor, each favor a renewal of the relationship, and each renewal a ratchet: no single moment produces a transfer of control that a journalist can photograph, but the cumulative result is a state whose economic policy, and increasingly its foreign policy, must be conducted with its creditor's preferences continuously in view. The debt trap, in its real rather than mythical form, looks less like Hambantota and more like Vientiane: not a seizure but an annuity of influence, collected at every rollover, forever.

The constellation of intermediate cases maps the range of the instrument. Djibouti is the one instance where the strategic-asset fear has partial substance: the country sits in external debt distress by the IMF's assessment, with China its largest creditor and investor, and in 2017, at the height of its borrowing, it became the site of China's first overseas military base, minutes from the Doraleh container terminal financed by a $344 million Export-Import Bank loan [19]. The base cannot be shown to be a debt-for-asset exchange, and no evidence has surfaced that it was; but a state whose fiscal survival depends on its creditor's patience is not well placed to refuse that creditor a naval facility, and it did not.

![The container terminal at the Port of Djibouti (photo: Skilla1st, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/85/The_container_terminal_at_the_Port_of_Djibouti.jpg)

Pakistan illustrates the opposite pole, influence through rescue rather than foreclosure: China has become Islamabad's lender of last resort, rolling over deposits and maintaining a currency swap line that reached $30 billion, while Pakistan, chronically short of dollars, has asked Beijing, Riyadh, and Abu Dhabi to roll some $12 billion a year in bilateral debt for three to five years, and has fallen Rs 423 billion behind on payments to Chinese power plants built under the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor [20]. Each rollover is granted, never guaranteed, and the perpetual possibility of refusal disciplines Pakistani policy more reliably than any garrison. Montenegro shows how even the myths carry information: a highway contract clause widely reported in the West as mortgaging Montenegrin territory to China proved on examination to be a waiver of sovereign immunity over state assets, aggressive but conventional, and the error mattered less than what it revealed, that Western publics were prepared to believe the contracts contained territorial collateral because so little else about them was knowable [21]. And in Tajikistan, where Beijing holds more than half of all foreign debt, the debt-adjacent transfers have already occurred: 1,122 square kilometers of Pamir mountain territory ceded in 2011, a major silver mine handed to a Chinese developer after less than thirty minutes of parliamentary debate [22]. No analyst has shown that either transfer was an explicit debt settlement. That is the point. When dependence is total, the transfers do not need to be itemized on the loan agreement.

The theory that unifies these cases begins from a problem in contract economics. Sovereign lending is the paradigmatic incomplete contract: no court can seize a state's assets, sovereign immunity shields the borrower, and reputation alone must enforce repayment. Western official creditors solved this problem collectively, through the Paris Club's shared discipline and the IMF's conditionality, accepting diluted returns in exchange for systemic stability. China solved it individually, by writing self-enforcing contracts: escrowed revenues held beyond the borrower's reach, confidentiality that multiplies the lender's informational advantage, cross-default clauses that make a missed payment a diplomatic incident, and an absolute refusal of the collective forums in which claims are pooled and diluted [7]. The result is a claim that behaves, in distress, less like a bond than like an equity option on the borrower's strategic assets and foreign policy. The option is almost never exercised; it does not need to be. Its value is realized at every rollover, every deferral, every refinancing negotiation in which the borrower must weigh its creditor's wishes, and the creditor, precisely because it has preserved principal at par and never written down a claim, never runs out of options to sell. This is why the haircuts of 2023 and 2024 matter beyond their sums: they established, in the first real test, that the new creditor order can impose its losses on private markets while the official claims, and the leverage attached to them, survive intact (see Figure 2).

None of this is illegal, and that is the final sense in which the imperialism is non-contact. The international legal order has no sovereign bankruptcy court, no collective mechanism binding on a creditor that declines to join it, and no category for the accumulation of influence through contract terms. When the UN General Assembly adopted basic principles for sovereign debt restructuring in 2015, by a vote of 136 to 6, the principles were hortatory, and the states that voted against them included the major private-creditor jurisdictions; the system as it exists protects no borrower and binds no creditor [23]. International law was built to police the seizure of territory by force, and it remains fluent in that language: the enduring outrage over Hambantota, an event that, as the documentary record shows, did not happen as described, demonstrates how readily the old categories seize on the wrong evidence. Meanwhile the actual instruments of twenty-first-century creditor power, the escrow account, the confidentiality clause, the comparability veto, the perpetual rollover, operate in a juridical vacuum, legible to no court and attributable to no act of state.

The corrective to the myth, then, is not absolution but sharper attention. The debt-trap narrative did the West a disservice twice over: first by being wrong, which costs credibility; and then by directing policy toward a fantasy of port seizures while the real competition proceeded in contract boilerplate and committee procedure, venues where a different and winnable contest was available. Borrower agency is part of the true story as well: Sri Lanka shopped its port, Laos chose its creditor, Zambia's committee has a French co-chair because the G20 built one, and the countries that have fared best are those that entered the negotiation with their books open and their options plural. Transparency is the cheapest counter-strategy on offer, since secrecy is the substrate on which every other mechanism feeds. But the deepest lesson belongs to history. The creditors of the Ottoman Empire required a foreign bureaucracy of thousands to take the revenues of a sovereign state. Their successors require a bank account, a clause, and a seat, or a conspicuously empty chair, at the restructuring table. Empire has not ended. It has been refinanced.
